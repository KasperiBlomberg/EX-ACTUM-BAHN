The job in one paragraph

Every few minutes a dealer asks for a price to move a car. We have 15 minutes to answer. If we win, we must find a freelance driver through the drivers' app by posting a gig with a fee, and the car must arrive by its deadline. A late car refunds part of the price and counts against the 90% on-time minimum that decides whether we are ranked at all. Score is gross margin: prices in, fees and refunds out. So each request is one question: is this car worth winning, and at what price, given how likely it is that a driver takes it in time?

The pipeline for one request

  1. Read the note.

    Dealers write notes in Finnish, Swedish or English: "valmis huomenna klo 10", "nouto ennen 16", "manual gearbox", "no plates". A rule-based reader turns these into a ready time, pickup and delivery hours, a tighter deadline, and flags. It gets about 95% of them right (measured against the reveal). The world obeys the note whether or not we read it, so a missed note means a failed visit we pay for.

  2. Apply the hard filters.

    Decline outright if the drive is over 450 km, if the slack is under 6 hours (10 hours for private addresses), or if it is a trade-in with a private address. Slack is the time between when the car can be collected and the last minute a pickup still makes the deadline, after subtracting the drive. Declining means quoting five times the list price, which costs nothing (an unanswered request costs goodwill).

  3. Estimate the risk that no driver takes the car.

    Three parts, combined as independent risks. The town part is a running success rate per pickup town, started from a prior based on how many drivers live within 30 km, and made worse by our own unbooked cars waiting in that town. The structural part adds risk for short slack (under 8 h: +20%; under 12 h: +7%) and long drives (over 300 km: +14%; over 200 km: +4%).

  4. Pick the price.

    For every markup from 1.00× to 2.30× the list price, in steps of 0.02, compute P(win) from the competitor model and the expected margin if won, and quote the markup with the best product. If the best expected value is zero or negative, decline. If our projected late share is already above 7.5% and this car is risky, decline too (the "brake"). All options we accept get the same markup; slower options are offered only when the fast one is much riskier.

  5. Post the gig and raise the fee on a schedule.

    Open at 0.92× the typical fee plus 0.30× the risk; step to 1.25× seven hours before the last safe pickup and 1.5× at 4.5 hours. After a drop or a no-show, raise at once. From the server's three-hour warning, go steep: 1.5× rising to 2.5× in half-hour steps.

  6. Learn.

    Each win or loss updates the competitor model. A booking before the warning marks the town "ok"; a warning, or four daytime hours unbooked, marks it "fail". Each on-time delivery raises that dealer's trust (they accept 1% more per order, up to 25%); a late one halves it. The running late share scales a penalty on risky cars.

The pricing model, interactively

The competitor is a hidden price per request: the list price times a world level (26% to 54% above list at level 8), a daily factor, and a lane factor. We never see it. We see only whether each quote won or lost, so the bot keeps a probability distribution over the world level and today's factor on a 37×31 grid and updates it after every result (a Bayesian grid posterior). The chance of winning at a given markup is a logistic curve centred on the competitor's price, with slope k = 11.

Drag the sliders. The left chart is what the bot believes about winning; the right chart is the margin it expects, and the dot is the price it quotes.

EV(u) = P(win | u) × ( u·list − f₀·fee − pFail·λ·(fee + 0.15·u·list) )
f₀ = 0.92 + 0.30·pFail    λ = 1 + 250·max(0, lateShare − 0.06)    quote = argmax over u ∈ {1.00, 1.02, …, 2.30}

Two things this shows. First, the quoted markup sits well below the competitor's price, because a 90% chance of a good margin beats a 50% chance of a slightly better one. Second, risk does not lower the price much; it mostly lowers the expected value until it crosses zero and the car is declined. That is why our order count is low: the decline is the bot's main risk control.

The fee schedule for one car

The log found that 93% of the cars we take are booked at the opening fee, and that paying more barely helps the rest: the cars that never book are limited by where drivers live and by timing, not by price. So the fee is not where the money is. Selection and price are.

What we could change

Ordered by how much margin each idea could plausibly add against how risky it is to build in a day. The first two are where the leaders' advantage most likely comes from.

  • 1. Bundle cars into one gig

    impact higheffort high

    The bot posts every car as its own gig. The rules allow one driver to take several cars in one gig, up to 12 hours of driving, and a driver's cost is dominated by getting to the pickup and home again, not by the drive itself. A return leg, a second car from the same yard, or two cars heading the same way cost a driver little extra. Bundled gigs book at lower fees and in towns where single cars don't book at all. This also handles the weekly bulk order (30–40 cars from one yard) that a one-gig-per-car bot cannot. The handbook calls this "chains of gigs" and says it is how a dispatcher wins.

  • 2. Win more cars: relax the filters where the risk model says it's safe

    impact higheffort low

    We decline about half of all requests up front. The hard limits (450 km, 6 h slack) predate the structural risk model, which now prices the same things. Setting G (550 km, backlog 12) already made +€400 a week with less buffer. A sweep on unseen seeds v-9 to v-16 over maxKm, minSlack and targetLate is cheap and runs unattended. The constraint is the worst-seed on-time share, not the average.

  • 3. Price each delivery option separately

    impact mediumeffort medium

    Dealers take same-day 84% of the time when both options cost the same, so same-day is worth more to them. Charging it more and next-day less steers the risky, tight-slack cars toward the option with more slack while earning more on the rest. Needs the competitor update to use the chosen option's price.

  • 4. A per-dealer offset in the competitor model

    impact mediumeffort medium

    Every morning the competitor lowers its prices for dealers where we won most and raises them where we won few. Our model has one world level and one day factor and so is slow to notice. A small per-dealer correction, learned from that dealer's wins and losses, follows it.

  • 5. Direct offers at the right fee, not the current one

    impact mediumeffort medium

    The earlier attempt sent offers to drivers finishing nearby at the fee already in the app, and 3 of 1,274 were accepted. The handbook says offers pay off on gigs whose fee drivers expect to rise: offer a fee above the app's, to the driver who is already in that town, before the warning.

  • 6. Use the snow forecast

    impact loweffort low

    A forecast event announces snow. Drivers there drop bookings and summer-tyre cars cannot be collected. Raising the risk for those towns and hours for that day is a few lines.

What not to spend time on: the fee schedule (most cars book at the opening fee), and the note reader (already 95%; remaining misses are rare phrasings). A better reader is worth maybe a handful of failed visits a week.

The €200 of Verda compute

Honest assessment: the bottleneck is not computation. The server allows eight practice worlds at a time and rate-limits requests, so a sweep is bound by the server, not by our machine. There is no local simulator to train a model against, and the rule-based parts (notes, pricing) are not where the margin is. A GPU would sit idle. Three uses do make sense:

  1. A machine that runs the final. The final is 34 real minutes on Sunday morning with a moving clock; a request has 4.5 real seconds. A cloud VM with a wired connection, running python -m spread_bot --final under nohup, does not sleep, lose wifi or get closed by accident. Rehearse it in Saturday's test finals (14:00 and 20:00 Helsinki). Keep a laptop ready as the second runner; two systems on one key share the request budget and take turns.
  2. Unattended overnight sweeps. Validate F and G and the relaxed filters of idea 2 on v-9…v-24 while we sleep, eight worlds at a time, and write the results to a file. CPU only.
  3. An LLM pass over the reveal notes, offline. Pull the notes from our played worlds, let a model label the ones the rule reader got wrong, and add them to the test cases. Small, optional, and never in the live loop: the rule reader answers in microseconds and the final allows seconds.

Do not plan around the credits; plan around the server's eight slots and Sunday's clock.
